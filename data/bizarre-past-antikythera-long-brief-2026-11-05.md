## Original English narration (~340 words, intended ~2:40–3:10 with expressive pauses; record, time and trim, do not pad)
“**Did you know…** this rusted lump of bronze once helped people predict eclipses?

Imagine diving beside a shipwreck in the Mediterranean. The divers find statues, precious objects, and a corroded piece of metal. It looks like junk. But inside that lump is something nobody expected.

Teeth. Dozens of tiny bronze gear teeth, arranged in an intricate machine.

It came from a shipwreck near the Greek island of Antikythera, recovered in the early twentieth century. The device itself had been made around two thousand years earlier, in the ancient Greek world.

For decades, its true purpose was a mystery. Why would an ancient machine need so many gears?

Researchers eventually used advanced imaging to see the parts hidden in the corrosion. They found that turning a hand-driven mechanism had once moved pointers across carefully calculated dials.

One dial followed a nineteen-year lunar calendar. Another traced a longer cycle of **two hundred and twenty-three lunar months** — the Saros cycle. Those repeating patterns allowed its owner to work out when solar or lunar eclipses might occur.

Picture that. No batteries. No computer chips. Just bronze gears converting the motion of one handle into astronomical predictions.

The mechanism also tracked other cycles, including the timing of major ancient Greek athletic contests. Later researchers proposed models showing how the missing front might have displayed the Sun, Moon, and planets. But those models are reconstructions, not surviving parts of a complete machine.

The builder’s identity is still unknown. We do not have the whole device, and we cannot claim to know exactly what every missing gear looked like.

So what is the real mystery?

Not whether aliens gave the Greeks forbidden technology. There is no need for that story.

The astonishing truth is what human engineers accomplished with mathematics, metal, and imagination — centuries before anyone could build an electronic computer.

A rusted fragment from the seafloor is proof that the ancient world could calculate the heavens by turning a handle.”
