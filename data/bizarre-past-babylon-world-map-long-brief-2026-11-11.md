## ENGLISH NARRATION v1 — 328 spoken words, naturally ~2:35–3:00 including meaningful pauses (adjust to finished recording, not mechanical quotas)
> Did you know a Babylonian map showed mysterious lands beyond the sea?
>
> More than two thousand five hundred years ago, someone carved an extraordinary picture onto clay. It was small enough to fit into a hand. But it tried to show an entire world.
>
> Look closely at the circle. Inside it are places the Babylonians knew. The great city of Babylon appears as a rectangle, beside the Euphrates. Other regions are named too, including Assyria and Elam.
>
> Now follow the ring around them. Its label means the Salt Sea. The known world seems surrounded by water. That is already very different from a map on your phone.
>
> But the most curious details are outside the ring.
>
> Little triangles point away from the sea. Scholars interpret them as distant regions, or islands. There were probably eight in the original drawing, although the tablet is damaged. Some inscriptions are missing entirely.
>
> And the writing is not simply a list of roads or distances. The surviving text mixes geography with descriptions of places far beyond familiar cities. Museum scholars explain that strange creatures and heroic figures appear in this imagined outer world.
>
> So, did Babylonians really believe someone could sail there and find monsters? We cannot know that from the broken tablet. Nor is this proof that a mythical journey actually happened.
>
> The object was found at ancient Sippar, in what is now Iraq. The surviving tablet is usually dated to around the sixth century before the Common Era.
>
> The mystery is what the triangles meant to the people who drew them.
>
> They marked a boundary between a recognizable homeland and a faraway world where stories, uncertainty, and geography could blend.
>
> On this little piece of clay, Babylon wasn't just a place on the map. It was a way of seeing the world — and everything beyond it.

## Historical accuracy / sources
- British Museum's museum-authored explanation (Smarthistory): https://smarthistory.org/map-of-the-world/ . **Core source**: c. sixth century BCE clay map from Abu Habba/Sippar; Babylon rectangle; Assyria/Elam named; surrounding “Salt-Sea”; outer region/island triangles probably originally eight; myth and familiar geography, incomplete inscription.
- British Museum collection record, object 1882,0714.509 / BM 92687: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1882-0714-509
- British Museum-affiliated reproductions and same commentary: https://www.khanacademy.org/humanities/ancient-art-civilizations/ancient-near-east1/babylonian/a/map-of-the-world
- Secondary evidence and chronological summary: https://www.worldhistory.org/image/526/babylonian-map-of-the-world/
**Claims boundary:** Do not say the triangles definitively represent monsters, that the tablet gives coordinates to Noah's Ark, that the tablet proves Biblical events, that this is unquestionably the world's oldest *map* of any kind, or that its geographic entities are modern cartographic equivalents. The object is fragmentary; explanations are scholarly interpretation. All AI-generated clay/characters/maps are reconstructions, not footage of the original tablet.
