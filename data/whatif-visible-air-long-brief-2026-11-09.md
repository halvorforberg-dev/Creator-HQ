## Full English scene-by-scene narration and visual actions
### 1. 00:00–00:14
**Voice:** What if you could SEE air? This plain cup seems perfectly still. But look through a special camera. Suddenly, a faint river climbs above it. Is the air different, or are we finally detecting it?
**Visual consequence:** FRAME ZERO: clear 16:9 cup on a table, natural warm light, no visible steam or special glow. By 00:01: split screen keeps the identical cup on left and a **labeled scientific visualization** on right; thin black/white rising gradient shifts upward. First-frame text 'WHAT IF WE COULD SEE AIR?' is readable on mobile. Match the right-side visualization and its motion to the question; this is a demonstration of an optical technique, **not** literally colored air or supernatural eyesight. Continue the same cup/lighting continuity into scene 2.

### 2. 00:14–00:34
**Voice:** We think of air as empty space because light usually passes right through it. But warm and cold patches can have different densities, and that slightly bends light. We normally miss those tiny distortions.
**Visual consequence:** Cup-to-hand split screen. Overlay moving air gradients while narrator describes temperature/density. Each named effect directly changes illustration.

### 3. 00:34–00:54
**Voice:** Scientists use a clever technique called schlieren imaging. Mirrors and a carefully positioned edge turn those tiny changes in light direction into visible bright and dark streaks. It's not magic vision: it's an optical measurement.
**Visual consequence:** Simple original animated light-ray diagram: straight ray through uniform air; bent ray through warm patch; edge blocks altered ray; real-style grayscale streak appears.

### 4. 00:54–01:15
**Voice:** Try the idea with a candle. Around the flame, warmer air rises. The camera can reveal a twisting plume climbing upward, even where there is no smoke to see. Now place a gentle draft nearby. The whole shape bends sideways.
**Visual consequence:** Live-style candle normal view → labeled illustrative schlieren view; fan turns and plume bends to show cause and effect. Never show smoke as the measured density gradient.

### 5. 01:15–01:36
**Voice:** Here's the surprise: you don't even need fire. A warm hand can make the nearby air move. The plume is subtle, but under the right conditions it can appear in a schlieren image. Your own body quietly changes the air around you.
**Visual consequence:** Hand near neutral patterned backdrop; warm plume shown as grayscale optical visualization and stays visually separate from actual colour vision.

### 6. 01:36–01:55
**Voice:** What about the blast from a hair dryer? It's suddenly easy to follow. One jet pushes across the screen, disturbs the warm plume and carries the pattern away. The science becomes a little moving map, not a collection of random swirls.
**Visual consequence:** Hair dryer begins; invisible jet rendered as contrasting wavefront/heat gradient; existing hand plume visibly displaced. No claims measuring absolute wind speed from shades alone.

### 7. 01:55–02:16
**Voice:** And then there is an extreme version. When an airplane flies faster than sound, it compresses air into sharp shock waves. NASA has photographed those otherwise invisible structures using background-oriented schlieren techniques.
**Visual consequence:** Original plane animation with expanding shockwave contours and matching background distortion. 'NASA photographed' accompanied by sourced static image only if usage rights cleared; otherwise original labeled diagram.

### 8. 02:16–02:34
**Voice:** One photograph looks as though the plane is cutting through glass. But nothing solid is breaking. The compressed air changes how light travels, and cameras reveal where the distortion is strongest.
**Visual consequence:** Background grid deforms as plane crosses; reset to normal photographic view; annotation says DENSITY CHANGES, NOT CRACKED AIR.

### 9. 02:34–02:53
**Voice:** There is a catch. Schlieren pictures don't reveal every breeze, every molecule or the true colour of air. They mainly highlight changes in density that bend light. Different setups show different kinds of detail.
**Visual consequence:** Side-by-side: a uniform moving air stream remains nearly invisible; a warm/cold boundary appears. Remove artificial neon 'wind trails'; add brief 'visualization, not literal sight' caption.

### 10. 02:53–03:16
**Voice:** Now look at that ordinary cup again. A moment ago it seemed completely still. Switch on the optical trick: a soft plume climbs, a draft nudges it sideways, and the air around the room becomes a living pattern. The air was moving all along. The amazing part is that we found a way to see its fingerprints.
**Visual consequence:** Final 23s single continuous split-screen: normal cup (left) versus accurate style Schlieren visualization (right); fan moves plume. Final title payoff visibly demonstrated; hold 2s on split-screen with final narration complete.
