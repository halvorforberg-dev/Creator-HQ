## Physics assumptions and checks

The Sun remains in place with the same mass and gravitational field, but its emission of *visible sunlight and heating radiation* is artificially switched off for exactly 24 hours at the source. It is a fictional thought experiment, not a possible physical switch; avoiding "Sun disappeared" wording is crucial because removing its mass would change Earth's gravitational orbit. After switch-off, old light continues to reach Earth for roughly 8 minutes 20 seconds (average), and the restored light arrives with the same delay. NASA says Earth receives sunlight in ~8 light-minutes [NASA Science](https://science.nasa.gov/exoplanets/what-is-a-light-year/); Smithsonian confirms ~8m20s [Smithsonian](https://howthingsfly.si.edu/ask-an-explainer/how-long-does-it-take-light-sun-get-earth); Caltech discusses the separate "Sun stops shining" case, temperature changes over **days to a week**, not instant global freezing [Caltech](https://www.caltech.edu/about/news/caltech-question-month-if-sun-ceased-exist-right-now-how-long-would-mankind-survive-would). The Moon dims after the last reflected sunlight reaches us. Plants lose photosynthetic light while the switch is off but do not universally die from one day. Climate/temperature changes vary greatly; NO invented exact global temperature drop, death count or cascading grid collapse. Solar power output falls where dependent on sunlight; alternatives/storage vary.

## English narration draft v1 (approx. 3–4 minutes; read aloud and trim naturally)

**[0:00–0:15 | Sun abruptly goes dark, hard cut to bright Earth and an eight-minute clock]**
What if the Sun switched OFF for twenty-four hours? Would Earth freeze, or could we survive one day without our star? Here's the first twist: when the Sun goes dark, nobody on Earth notices. Not immediately.

**[0:15–0:35 | Photons streaming through space, timer 08:20]**
Sunlight takes roughly eight minutes and twenty seconds to reach us. So for a little while, we keep receiving light that already left the Sun. The sky still looks blue. Morning continues. And that bright sunset you see? It comes from a star that's already switched off.

**[0:35–0:56 | Sunlit half of globe flips to night; city lights glow]**
Then the last sunlight passes Earth. Everywhere facing the Sun, day becomes night. It isn't total blackness: stars, streetlights and windows still shine. But the familiar blue daylight is gone. The Moon, lit by the Sun, loses its glow too after the last reflected light arrives.

**[0:56–1:20 | Sun-powered farm slows; batteries and power plants pick up]**
Solar panels stop generating electricity while the light is missing. Places with batteries and other sources of power can keep running; places that depend heavily on sunshine face a sudden problem. And people who expected sunrise? They might wake up to a sky that never brightens.

**[1:20–1:43 | Flowers close, birds settle, stars over daytime city]**
Nature gets a strange extra night. Daytime animals may change their routines, and plants stop making sugars from sunlight. But a single day without light does not instantly kill every tree or crop. Living things have reserves, just as they survive ordinary nights.

**[1:43–2:12 | Thermometer over sea, land, houses; orbit diagram unchanged]**
Now the question everyone asks: wouldn't the planet freeze? Not in an instant. Oceans, ground and atmosphere have stored heat. Temperatures would begin changing, with very different effects in different places, but the whole planet would not turn into ice after a few hours. And Earth would not fly off into deep space. The Sun is still there. Only its light has stopped, not its gravity.

**[2:12–2:40 | Time-lapse hour 12 to hour 24; humans look up at stars]**
Twelve hours later, people on the Sun-facing side are living through an impossible night. From the other side of Earth, it might feel like an ordinary night that simply lasts too long. Our clocks keep turning. The planet keeps spinning and orbiting. This is a crisis for systems built around daylight—not an instant end to the world.

**[2:40–3:10 | Sun lights up again; light-speed return traveling to Earth]**
Then, at hour twenty-four, the Sun switches back on. Does daylight instantly return? No. That new light still needs about eight minutes to travel here. And when it arrives, Earth's daytime sky lights up again. The planet was never a frozen rock. It survived a very strange extra night.

**[3:10–3:24 | Earth glowing, final words with clear graphic]**
So here's the surprising answer: one day without sunlight would disrupt life and power, but our world stores heat—and sunlight takes time to arrive. The Sun doesn't have to vanish to show us just how much we depend on it.
