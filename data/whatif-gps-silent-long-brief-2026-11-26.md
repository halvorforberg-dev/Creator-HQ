# What If GPS Went Silent for 24 Hours?
Editorial candidate for Sunday 29 Nov 18:00 Oslo, regular 16:9. Export deadline Fri 27 Nov 18:00; QA and actual booking by Sat 28 Nov 18:00. No booking yet.
Premise: all GPS signals become unavailable for 24h globally; other navigation constellations and local/inertial navigation, terrestrial networks and clocks remain unless individually dependent on GPS. No attack explanation. No claim every device, aircraft, phone, bank or grid fails; effect depends on backup and holdover.
Payoff: The more consequential dependency is an invisible common clock, not the map; GPS gives timing to distributed communications, financial and grid equipment, and backups determine whether a day-long outage is felt.
Titles A chosen: What If GPS Went Silent for 24 Hours? B: What If the World Lost GPS for One Day?
Hooks: A What if GPS went silent? (moving car approaches fork, driver stops safely); B What if your map lost you? (navigation screen/vehicle); C What if GPS disappeared for a day? (airport and phone). Choose A, no false promise of all satellites failing.
Original story and required shots; motion at every key consequence, generic footage for illustration, not false disaster coverage:
1. 0–20s: tracking shot car reaching unfamiliar junction, driver checks navigation when stopped; readable WHAT IF immediately, no intro. "What if GPS went silent for twenty-four hours? Your phone still works. The streets are still there. But the little dot telling you exactly where you are may freeze or drift. If your device can use other satellite systems, it might hardly notice. For equipment that relies on GPS alone, the day gets complicated."
2. 20–40s: moving cockpit and runway approach, alternate navigation and grounded plane/airport departure board, no falling aircraft. "At an airport, a pilot may lose a precision navigation option. Aircraft have other navigation systems, so this does not mean planes simply fall out of the sky. But some approaches may be unavailable, and some flights might have to divert or wait."
3. 40–60s: harbor traffic, ship bridge radar, cargo cranes and trucks navigating; visible radar screen not falsely identified as GPS. "At sea, crews can still use radar and other navigation tools. But coordinating routes and arrivals becomes harder for ships that depend on GPS. A single delayed ship can leave trucks and workers waiting for cargo."
4. 60–85s: real moving telecommunications tower maintenance, server racks, bank transaction processing; brief simple clocks graphic only if accurate. "Then comes a problem you can't see on a map. GPS satellites carry atomic clocks. Their signals tell equipment thousands of kilometres apart when the same instant happens. Mobile networks and financial systems use that shared timing. With the signal gone, backup clocks must take over."
5. 85–110s: moving substation equipment, engineer watching synchronized measurements, analog clock close up; no staged blackout. "Power companies also use precise timestamps to compare measurements across the grid. Local clocks don't instantly stop when GPS disappears. Some can stay accurate for hours or much longer. Others gradually drift. The longer the outage lasts, the more important those backups become."
6. 110–125s: return to moving car traveling safely, passenger looking at clock, city lights continue, music resolves. "By the end of the day, the worst disruption might not be a driver taking the wrong turn. It might be distant machines no longer agreeing on the same instant. GPS is not just a map in your pocket. It is also a clock the world shares."
