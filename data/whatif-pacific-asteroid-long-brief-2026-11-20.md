## Write the answer before the opening

**Answer / human payoff:** A 10-km asteroid impact in the deep Pacific would NOT be made harmless by the ocean. Severe near-impact effects and complex tsunami hazards could be followed by more widespread atmospheric, transport and food-security disruption. Exact tsunami heights, cooling and casualties require site-specific models and **must not** be presented as predictions. A person thousands of kilometres inland might survive the initial impact and still struggle with months of food and supply disruption. **This is a fictional extreme scenario, not a forecast of a real incoming asteroid; NASA currently reports no known significant threat for the coming century.**

**First two seconds curiosity:** How can a huge ocean fail to protect us from a huge asteroid? **Visible final answer:** inland household still needs food deliveries, beside impact wave map; `THE OCEAN IS NOT A SHIELD`. Open on the catastrophic impact, not a generic lead-in.

## Locked pre-production narration (373 spoken words, estimated ~3:05–3:30 with thoughtful pace)

**Scene 1 — 00:00–00:19:** What if a ten-kilometre asteroid struck the Pacific tomorrow? Look at that ocean. You might think billions of tons of water could cushion the blow. But at this size and speed, the ocean is no shield.

**Scene 2 — 00:19–00:40:** Imagine a rock the width of a city moving at twenty kilometres per second. Air cannot stop it. Water flashes into vapour around the impact. The shock punches through the sea and into the seafloor.

**Scene 3 — 00:40–01:01:** For a moment, a gigantic cavity opens in the ocean. Then the water falls back. That collapsing cavity launches waves across the basin. Not one perfect wall of water, but a complicated, terrifying series of surges.

**Scene 4 — 01:01–01:20:** Now the danger moves. Islands and coastlines face different waves at different times. Some locations might have hours of warning after the impact. Others could have much less. The shoreline and seafloor would decide how far the water reached.

**Scene 5 — 01:20–01:38:** But the waves are only the first act. Look up. The collision throws water, dust and shattered rock high into the sky. Some of the finest material could travel enormous distances.

**Scene 6 — 01:38–01:59:** Sixty-six million years ago, another huge impact helped end the age of dinosaurs. But that asteroid struck rocks rich in sulfur. A deep Pacific impact is not identical. We cannot simply copy its exact darkness, temperature drop, or tsunami map.

**Scene 7 — 01:59–02:19:** Even so, an impact this powerful could disrupt climate, shipping and food production on a global scale. How badly depends on the impact angle, materials and location. Those uncertainties are real, not details to hide.

**Scene 8 — 02:19–02:40:** Picture a family thousands of kilometres inland. No giant wave reaches their street. Their windows survive the first blast. But days later, deliveries could slow. Food supplies could tighten. The threat can cross oceans without arriving as water.

**Scene 9 — 02:40–03:02:** That is the twist. The dramatic first day could be deadly near the impact. Yet for people far away, the longer problem might be feeding millions through a damaged global system. Survival is not just about escaping the first wave.

**Scene 10 — 03:02–03:25:** So, could the ocean save us from a dinosaur-killing asteroid? No. Water changes the disaster. It does not erase it. If you live far from the shore, you might survive the impact itself, and still spend months fighting for the basics of life.
